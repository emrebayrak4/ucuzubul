## Why visit Rome

Rome is an open-air history book. Ancient ruins sit beside Renaissance churches and baroque fountains, and you can stand where emperors, popes, and artists left their mark in a single afternoon. The food is simple and memorable, centered on pasta, pizza, and gelato, and the city's piazzas come alive in the evening. Rome also contains Vatican City, home to some of the most celebrated art and architecture in the world.

## Top sights and experiences

- **Colosseum** — The iconic amphitheater of ancient Rome.
- **Roman Forum and Palatine Hill** — The ruins of the ancient city's political and residential heart.
- **Vatican Museums and Sistine Chapel** — A vast art collection culminating in Michelangelo's ceiling.
- **St. Peter's Basilica** — A monumental church with a climbable dome and city views.
- **Pantheon** — A remarkably preserved ancient temple with an open oculus.
- **Trevi Fountain** — Baroque drama; visit early morning to avoid crowds.
- **Piazza Navona** — A lively square built on the footprint of an ancient stadium.
- **Borghese Gallery** — Bernini sculptures and Caravaggio paintings, by reservation.
- **Trastevere evenings** — Cobblestone lanes full of trattorias and bars.
- **Appian Way** — An ancient road lined with ruins, nice by bike.

## Where to stay: best neighborhoods

**Centro Storico** puts you steps from the Pantheon, Piazza Navona, and Campo de' Fiori. It is ideal for first-timers who want to walk everywhere, but it is busy and rooms can be small.

**Trastevere** charms with ivy-covered buildings and a strong dining and bar scene. It suits couples and foodies, though it can be noisy late and has limited Metro access.

**Monti** is a hip, compact area near the Colosseum with boutiques and wine bars. It is central and characterful, with some steep streets.

**Prati** sits near the Vatican and feels residential and orderly, with good restaurants and Metro links. Nightlife is quieter.

**Termini** (around the main train station) is practical for transit and often good value, but some streets feel less pleasant at night, so choose your block carefully.

## Things to know before you go

- **Entry:** Italy is part of the Schengen Area. US citizens can generally visit for short stays without a visa, but new EU entry systems are being introduced. Check official sources before you travel.
- **Dress codes:** Churches, including St. Peter's, require covered shoulders and knees. Carry a light scarf.
- **Tipping:** Not expected as in the US. Many restaurants include a cover charge ("coperto"). Rounding up or leaving a little extra for good service is a kind gesture.
- **Coffee culture:** Standing at the bar is quick and usually cheaper than table service.
- **City tax:** Hotels charge a per-night tourist tax, often collected at the property.
- **Safety:** Rome is generally safe, but pickpocketing is common on crowded buses and at major sights. Be cautious of anyone offering "free" bracelets, roses, or photos who then asks for money. Check official government travel advisories before your trip.
- **Respect monuments:** Sitting on some famous steps and wading in fountains is prohibited and can bring fines.

## Getting around

Central Rome is best explored on foot, though cobblestones make comfortable shoes essential. The Metro has limited lines because of archaeological remains underground, so buses and trams fill the gaps. Validate paper tickets when boarding, or use contactless payment where accepted. Licensed taxis are white with a roof sign; use official ranks or apps. Trains connect Fiumicino Airport with the city center, and buses serve Ciampino.

## Best time to visit

Spring and fall bring warm, comfortable weather that is ideal for walking. Summer is hot and crowded, and many locals leave the city in August. Winter is mild and quieter, with fewer lines at the major sights, though some days are rainy.

## Budget tips

- Book Colosseum and Vatican tickets online in advance to avoid lines and resale markups.
- Many churches, including ones with major artworks, are free to enter.
- Refill water bottles at the public drinking fountains around the city.
- Eat where locals do, a few streets away from major landmarks.
- Try pizza al taglio (by the slice) or supplì for a filling, low-cost lunch.
- Consider visiting state museums on any designated free-admission days, checking official sites for details.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many days do I need in Rome?

Three to four days covers the ancient sites, Vatican, and historic center. Five or more allows for slower exploring and day trips.

### Can I visit the Vatican and Rome on the same trip without extra paperwork?

Yes. Vatican City is entered freely from Rome, with no separate border formalities for visitors.

### Is it safe to drink from Rome's public fountains?

The small public drinking fountains, called "nasoni," provide potable water. Ornamental fountains are not for drinking or wading.

### Should I rent a car in Rome?

Generally no. The historic center has restricted traffic zones, and driving and parking are stressful. Public transit and walking work better.
